Origins of Japanese martial arts, Jutsu vs Do

Provisional English translation — awaiting review and approval.

Réal Genest Sensei — Originally published: 2017-10-26

Bujutsu: martial arts

Bu: 武 defines the military sphere

Jutsu: 術 techniques, art

Shi: 士 person; Bushi 武 士, a person who practises the profession of arms, a member of the warrior class of the 12th century. Bujutsu was a system of study and training devised by warriors, for warriors, and reserved for this military brotherhood. A curriculum of study and practice in 18 disciplines provided the training of the bushi: Bugei juhappan (武芸十八). This military structure remained in force until the beginning of the Edo era, 1603–1867.

Kobudo: traditional martial way 古武道

Ko: 古 ancient, traditional, classical

Do: 道 way, path

With the arrival of Edo and the Tokugawa family, the importance of warriors would decline considerably. The demobilization of numerous armies led to unemployment aggravated by poverty within the military profession. Members of the bugei class would take administrative positions in the new government. Following the predominance of military culture, Edo would see the urban culture of ordinary people flourish. This transition would encourage the emergence of a spiritual and artistic culture.

Ordinary people would develop their arts and begin to reflect on spirituality. There would be a transition from “jutsu” to “do”: kenjutsu to kendo, Iai jutsu to Iaido, Bujutsu to Budo…

Budo: martial way, 武道

According to ancient texts, the term budō defined the manner of governing the country and nation through a military government. Warriors were subject to an ethical code, Bushido 武士道, “the way of the warrior”. Over time, the way of the warrior, which nurtures the warrior's spirit in the face of death, would become a national ideal. In the practice of Kobudō, the search for the warrior's spirit would become a principal concern.

The lethal effectiveness of combat disciplines would give way to individual fulfilment centred on spiritual awakening, a spiritual fusion with the cosmic whole. Zen, Shintoism and Oyomei Confucianism, Wang Yang-ming, would inspire the various thinkers of the ryū during this era of transition. The intellect plays with concepts, but only intuition, “Kan”, understands their deeper meaning. A long succession of masters of arms would guide this aesthetic and spiritual movement.

Sports: Karate dō, judo, shinai geiko…

In the great public confusion surrounding Japanese combat disciplines, we must point to teachers of Japanese disciplines who know nothing of Japanese culture, and to news media that use the term “martial arts” indiscriminately. Disciplines born from the Meiji era, 1867, onward were grouped into martial ways or combat sports.

Karate dō devoted exclusively to spiritual exploration is a Budo, whereas if the discipline becomes an instrument of competition centred on trophies, we are speaking of sport. In the way of the sword, shinai geiko is sporting fencing, and it bears no resemblance to traditional Kendo. ― Réal Genest Sensei
