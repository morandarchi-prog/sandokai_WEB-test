Sandokai — 參同契

Provisional English translation — awaiting review and approval.

Réal Genest Sensei — Originally published: 2017-10-26

SANDOKAI is said to be the essence of Buddhism. Understanding SANDOKAI means gaining an understanding of Kobudô, the traditional Japanese martial ways. The disciplines of Kobudô pursue the same objective as Zen monks: spiritual fulfilment, or “Satori”. Without this fulfilment, practising a Kobudô discipline is nothing more than a game of situations, a theatre of nostalgic warriors, or a gathering of members of an Orientalizing social club.

For Westerners who know nothing of the fundamental elements of Japanese culture and its traditions, Kobudô disguises itself as “martial arts”, and the exoticism of the samurai heightens their warrior fantasies.

Sandokai is not a discipline; it is an instrument, a beacon on the Way. Sandokai indicates the Way to follow; the disciple continues the search. Through the study and practice of Jodo, traditional Kendo and Iaido, students explore the psychological and spiritual aspects illuminated by Sandokai.

Thus, SAN encompasses our usual, everyday perceptions. We establish that there is us and there are others. There is a difference, a distance between others and us. SAN is this perception of differences that leads us to believe we are an entity in a universe occupied by other entities. This deceptive outlook leads us to believe that we are isolated, self-sufficient entities. Our belief in an individual “soul” results from such a limited vision.

From this perspective, our fragmented consciousness assesses the world and our relationships with it.

In Kobudô, SAN's fragmentation of reality can take several forms. We may establish that there is us and our training partner, or us and the weapon with which we train. In traditional Kendo practice, the Bokken, the wooden sword, is apparently an entity separate from us. All basic training aims to familiarize us with this instrument and make it part of us, an extension of our body.

With time and determination, the body accepts the weapon. Differences disappear. We become “ONE” with our weapon. Occasionally during practice, there comes a moment when we commit to an attack and, at that instant, lose all notion of our environment, our weapon and ourselves. For a fraction of a second or a very short time, we are no longer aware of making the movement or holding the sword.

Usually, this moment of perfect symbiosis is carried by a Kiai, a shout. The shout's function is to fuse all the elements — mind, sword and body — into a single momentum. In the attack, there is no longer consciousness or sword, and we lose all notion of our being; what is expressed is the energy of the three elements, Ki. This sublime instant is called DO. This DO must not be confused with “Dō”, meaning the Way, the path.

During the DO phase, all arbitrary differences vanish, and the individual is entirely centred, rather than concentrated, in the movement. They are at once the body, the consciousness or energy animating the body, and the intended goal. It is as though all the various elements that occupied so much space in the brain were compressed into a tiny point. The usual functions of the brain's left hemisphere are deactivated.

These functions, which normally take up all the space and behave as though they were the only authentic functions of the human mind, are blocked and become mere spectators while the individual's full cosmic consciousness emerges from the right hemisphere and expresses itself in a perfect momentum.

This space, freed from the activity of fragmented consciousness, leaves room for the unifying manifestations of Intuition. Individuals realize that they are the energy of their sword, their body and their consciousness, and that these various energies are the same energy animating the Universe and Life within it.

Satori can be defined as the moment when individuals realize they are not the individual entity their fragmented consciousness has always led them to believe they were, but this total energy that emerges from their true consciousness at a moment when all conditions are met. Training aims to prepare individuals and bring together the conditions that will encourage the breaking of their shell and the emergence of their spiritual energy.

This instant that encourages the emergence of “Satori” or the grasping of “Kan”, cosmic intuition, is called spiritual Illumination. The usual, limited perception is overturned, making way for a new vision of one's relationship with Life, Death and the Universe.

When the experience of unified action ends, individuals return to their usual perception. Through sustained, repeated training, the experience manifests more frequently, and the perception of Reality becomes more stable and discerning. In their perception of the world, something within them has changed. This new outlook is KAI. SAN enters DO: this is KAI, the renewed and whole individual.

In traditional Kendo practice, masters often say that one must practise KI-KEN-TAI no Ichi:

• KI: vital energy, consciousness, spirit, “Feeling”…

• KEN: the sword, or any other weapon outside us, from which we are separate…

• TAI: the physical body and all its manifestations: movements, steps…

• No Ichi: in one, unified, a state of symbiosis…

The practice of Kobudô, illuminated by Sandokai, creates a situation encouraging students' intuitive development and spiritual harmony with Life. Kobudô taught according to the essence of Zen leads individuals to spiritual fulfilment. Beyond this fulfilment, nothing exists.

The world of Budo was in turmoil; the West had just discovered certain combat disciplines. In so-called “specialist” magazines and on television, Americans could be seen wearing ridiculous, extremely tasteless costumes, bestowing the title of “Grand Master” upon themselves and promoting the style they had just created. Everything Kobudô and Budo carried culturally, morally and spiritually was obscured and replaced by an ungainly, insulting circus.

The masters therefore concluded that “gaijin” were incapable of understanding Japanese “Seishin”. One American demonstrated with elegance and strength that a “gaijin” could encounter this cultural treasure and understand Japanese “kokoro”: Mr. Donn F. Draeger overturned all the prejudices surrounding “gaijin”. ― Réal Genest Sensei
