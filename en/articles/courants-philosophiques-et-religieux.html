Philosophical and religious currents

Provisional English translation — awaiting review and approval.

Réal Genest Sensei — Originally published: 2017-10-26

Buddhism

Buddhism began to spread in Japan during the Heian era, 794–1185, mainly through two major esoteric sects, Tendai, “Heavenly Terrace”, and Shingon, “True Word”. Facing death, warriors found psychological support in the magical tradition of this form of Buddhism. Magical characters taken from sutras and magical incantations of Shingonshu were engraved on blades.

Zen would become a more widespread practice among bushi and samurai with the arrival of the Edo period. Buddhism prepares individuals to understand their relationship with Life and death.

Shintoism

Shintoism defines the relationship of individuals with Nature and their environment. It is Japan's oldest tradition and dates back to the Jomon period. This pagan outlook inspired the Japanese reverence for Nature. In the eighth century, with the writing of the Kojiki, a new structure of Shintoism would be placed under the imperial family and the emperor, Tenno, as High Priest and master of rituals. Imperial Shintoism would diverge from the Shintoism of farmers.

These two facets of the same tradition would contribute to confusion within a tradition that originally involved contemplating the forces and energies sustaining Nature, the kami.

Taoism

Taoism, or Daoism, is more a logical explanation of the movements and energies that animate Nature. It also attempts to explain the relationship between Humanity, the Universe and Earth. Yin and Yang are already mentioned in the Dao De Jing, and the Theory of the Five Elements (Go Gyo) in the fourth century before our era. These principles were organized into a system during the Warring States period, with followers also relating the Five Elements to the Five Virtues.

This school of thought, called Inn-Yo in Japanese, greatly influenced Kobudo and traditional medicine. Daoism would inspire the term “Do”, the Way: Kobudō, kendo, judo, iaido…

Confucianism

Confucianism is an ethic of life defining a social order and relationships between individuals. It was developed in China in the sixth century of our era by the Chinese philosopher Confucius (Kôshi in Japanese), and reached Japan through Korea at the beginning of the sixth century… Confucianism enjoyed its heyday during the Edo era (1603–1867), when the shogunal power of the Tokugawa dynasty promoted it as Japan's ethical philosophy, establishing a kind of “state Confucianism”.

Chu Hsi or Zhu Xi. Confucianism was then taught in schools and political and military administrations as a moral code, shaping the mentality of several generations of samurai and intellectuals. Among the population, Wang Yang Ming gradually took root. This school encouraged intuition and moral sense. ― Réal Genest Sensei
