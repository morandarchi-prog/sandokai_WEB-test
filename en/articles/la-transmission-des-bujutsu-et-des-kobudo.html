The transmission of Bujutsu and Kobudo

Provisional English translation — awaiting review and approval.

Réal Genest Sensei — Originally published: 2017-11-22

Since its establishment as an institution (1192), Japanese Bujutsu has become increasingly removed from urban culture. Military arts occupied the entire cultural sphere. The military had seized political power, great clans governed different regions, and the Mikado, the Emperor and the nobility were reduced to symbolic power. In addition, Tenno, the Emperor, exercised and still exercises the role of High Priest of Shintoism.

Between the 12th and 17th centuries, military clans, the Buke, would confront one another and struggle for control of regions and ultimately for domination of all Japan. Military culture would spread through the study and practice of 18 martial disciplines, the Ju Happan.

In the beginning, military arts were passed down within families and extended families, then within the clan. Individuals who had distinguished themselves in combat, won several victories and inspired the clan's young people were offered positions as instructors when they decided to retire from military life.

The experience of these warriors, the Bushi, represented a priceless cultural wealth. Not only had they lived through the rigours of battlefields and all their horrors, but their survival in combat demonstrated that they had understood certain aspects of war and armed confrontation that escaped the vast majority of ordinary combatants.

Military power brought a degree of political power to the clans. Yet military and fighting strength depended on the education and quality of training of their young bushi warriors. What initially rested on the experience of a few outstanding individuals became a training system surrounded by secrecy. Clan members' survival depended on their ability to sense danger and manage the situation.

In bujutsu, the martial arts, a confrontation almost always led to the death of one combatant. These arts were designed to eliminate a danger rapidly.

Masters recorded their experience, knowledge, strategies and reflections in writing on makimono. These scrolls became the archives of the school or Ryū. When a successor to the head of the school was chosen, the master passed on to his disciple the technical and spiritual secrets framing the study of that school.

Technical errors were deliberately placed in the makimono to confuse anyone who might steal these documents and attempt to appropriate their knowledge. During oral transmission, from Soke to Deshi, the master instructed his successor in the secrets of the makimono and the Ryū.

“Contrary to popular belief, the exact sciences were already well established in Japan's feudal era. Although held in very low regard compared with moral studies, they were indispensable to samurai, who used them in fields as varied as artillery, castle construction, topography and astronomy.

Yet the teachings of the Koryū (ancient traditions) were never transmitted from a ‘scientific’ and ‘analytical’ perspective as these notions are commonly understood in the West.

Of course, bujutsu were created through observation and analysis, empirically. But although a set of forms can be studied through analysis, what makes them effective can only be grasped in its entirety through feeling.

The human body is incredibly complex, and an effective martial movement requires performing, simultaneously or at different times, in opposite or converging directions, too many actions for the brain to coordinate consciously. Logical study therefore leads to a dead end in practice, which is why the bushi never used it to transmit teachings vital to them.

Traditional transmission, by contrast, centred on feeling and intuition. This method of teaching is perfectly illustrated by the expression ‘i shin den shin’, soul to soul.” Léo Tamaki, Transmission in the martial arts

“This expression originating in Zen translates approximately as ‘soul to soul’ or ‘heart to heart’. It perfectly illustrates the nonverbal teaching that is the essence of the transmission of all ‘do’, whether in zen, chado, shodo, budo, etc.” Léo Tamaki, I shin den shin

We must understand that Bujutsu, the martial arts before Edo, are, in their traditional spirit, an anachronism in our time. Modern people seek answers, and if feudal ryū refuse to become more flexible and turn into living, dynamic instruments in this search, they are, like dogmatic religions, doomed to disappear. Several hundred ryū have already disappeared; perhaps they did not meet individuals' needs.

Other ryū, however, have questioned themselves and reworked their structures while preserving the traditional spirit, seeking to meet individuals' spiritual needs. These bujutsu continue to grow and transmit a cultural and spiritual treasure that enriches those who immerse themselves in it.

Unlike bujutsu, kobudō are an infinite source of intuitive and spiritual fulfilment for the individual. That said, although they are a wonderful instrument for meeting individuals' existential needs, the teacher must still have travelled the path, the Michi, and understand its Kokoro. There lies the profound secret of Kobudō: knowledge of techniques is merely the shell of the discipline. The ignorant person stuffs themselves with techniques and deprives themselves of the essential.

The kobudō born during Edo were deeply influenced by urban culture and the new current of Wáng Yángmíng 王陽明, Oyomei in Japanese, which centres its inquiry on the development of creativity and Intuition — Kan.

Shihan

In traditional groups, titles were simple: Sōke (宗家), the one who transmits the tradition, and Shihan (師範), a teacher or model master, a guiding light, the one who teaches the tradition. Nowadays, an individual who has studied a complex ryū, meaning a school that is a jutsu and encompasses several disciplines, must hold a Menkyō Kaiden to transmit a tradition with authorization.

In kobudō, traditional martial ways such as Iaido, the title Shihan authorizes transmission and recognizes that this teacher has studied and knows all the techniques and esoteric teachings of the ryuha.

The kobudō that emerged from traditional schools beginning in the Edo era, 1617, have no kyu-Dan grading system. The master, the Shihan, usually gives a written document to a student who has completed the study and acquired the technical competence of the Shoden, Chuden or Okuden level. Later, the student is awarded the title of teacher (Shihan) and authorization to transmit the School's corpus and spirit.

Masters often say: “Gaikoku no hitobito wa kobudo no Kokoro O rikaii suru no ga mutsukashi yo u desu (For most foreigners, the spirit of kobudō is difficult to grasp and understand).”

The tradition passes from the sensei's Kokoro to the student's Kokoro. This explains traditional masters' persistent refusal to transmit their art to Westerners infatuated with their intellect and reason. As a result, foreigners, “gaijin”, are directed toward sporting disciplines. All that is asked of them is performance, and in return they are given little gifts to encourage them: kyu and dan.

Even today in Japan, traditional masters teach small groups of a few dozen individuals. Selection is demanding: one must accept and be ready to question all one's convictions and perceptions. One must let go. Nothing is more uncertain than the world of Kobudō. We must return to the first teaching of the Buddha Sakyamuni to grasp its full immensity. Spiritual Awakening is the ultimate stage. ― Réal Genest Sensei
